# Literature for an AI-based comprehensive physical fitness evaluation program for pilots

Verified reading list, compiled 31 October 2026. 328 papers (20 core, 141 directly useful, 167 transferable), published 1973 to 2026.

Scope: international literature. Korean papers are excluded, meaning papers written in Korean or published in a Korean journal or as a Korean thesis. English-language papers by Korean research groups in international journals are kept.

The list covers the program's two focus areas:

1. Classifying pilots into aptitude-based physical types from muscular-strength indicators.
2. Decision-making support built on fitness evaluation results.

## Main findings

1. **No direct precedent was found.** No published study in this search applies machine learning, clustering or composite scoring to pilots' muscular-strength indicators to derive aptitude-based physical types. The search covered English, Korean and Chinese literature, including the Korean papers later removed from this list. A program that does this would be filling a gap, not repeating existing work.

2. **The closest work links physical measures to pilot outcomes with conventional statistics.**
   - In Finnish fighter pilots, selection-phase muscular fitness tests (pull-ups, back extension) were associated with later spinal-injury flight duty limitation.
   - Studies of military aircrew trainees model G tolerance from a wearable cardiac force index, and from sprint and jump performance.
   - A prospective Royal Australian Air Force cohort modelled neck-pain risk, with neck and trunk strength among the factors.
   - Studies of Korean Air Force cadets and pilots, published in international journals, relate isokinetic strength, body composition and Functional Movement Screen scores to centrifuge G-test pass or fail.

3. **Physical typing of pilots exists for body build, not for strength.**
   - A 2019 study classified aircraft-carrier pilots into somatotypes with discriminant functions; the muscular somatotype had the highest tolerance to motion stress.
   - US Navy and US Air Force reports use anthropometry, much of it multivariate, to set body-size limits for each aircraft and to assign candidates to training pipelines.
   - FAA and US Air Force studies compare control-force strength with aircraft control specifications.

4. **Methods for strength-based typing exist in military and sport populations, with warnings attached.**
   - Methods: k-means clustering of force-plate strength and power in US Marine Corps recruits; hierarchical clustering of Combat Fitness Test results in US Marines; latent profile analysis of trunk strength in athletes; force-velocity profile typologies.
   - Warnings: a randomised trial found training individualised by force-velocity type no better than standard training, and force-velocity slopes from jumping were unreliable.

5. **Decision-support precedents for pilot selection use machine learning on non-physical data.**
   - Examples: an explainable machine-learning model for US Air Force pilot candidate selection, embedded in the Pilot Applicant Selection System; machine-learning prediction of US Air Force pilot-training success; US Navy attrition-risk and aircraft-suitability models; and an AHP (analytic hierarchy process) model for medical selection of high-performance fighter pilots.
   - Strength-based role assignment has older, non-machine-learning precedents: the US Air Force Strength Aptitude Test (incremental lift) and the US Army's MEPSCAT and OPAT strength batteries.

6. **Expect weak signals, and validate types against outcomes.**
   - Fighter pilots' G-duration tolerance could not be predicted from anthropometric and physiological characteristics.
   - Fitness explained little of the variance in Israel Air Force pilot-selection scores.
   - In 963 Air Force personnel, force-plate jump scores were associated with injury, but no model classified injuries accurately.
   - Any strength-based typology should therefore be checked against outcomes such as G tolerance, training success and flight duty limitation. TRIPOD+AI, PROBAST+AI and DECIDE-AI (in the decision-support section) set the reporting and bias standards for such models.

## How the list was built

- **Search.** Crossref, OpenAlex, Europe PMC, PubMed and arXiv were searched through their public interfaces, plus web search, in English, Korean and Chinese. The work was split into six research angles, followed by targeted searches on gaps and citation tracing from the strongest papers.
- **Screening.** About 1,900 unique records were seen and 634 candidates screened in.
- **Korean papers removed.** After rating, 12 papers written in Korean or published in Korean journals or as a Korean thesis were removed to keep the list international.
- **Rating.** Each candidate was rated from its abstract on the scale below. Every paper rated 4 or 5 then had an independent second review that tried to knock the rating down: 45 of 214 ratings were lowered, and the lower rating was kept. Core papers, and papers whose database record had no abstract, were checked by hand against their source abstracts.
- **Verification.** Every listed paper was re-confirmed in Crossref, OpenAlex or Europe PMC, and its bibliographic details come from those records. Retracted papers were excluded. Several mass-retracted "AI physical fitness evaluation" papers turned up during the search and were removed.
- **Summaries.** These describe the abstracts, not the full texts. Check the full text before citing specific numbers.

**Rating scale**

- **5, core.** Studies pilots, aircrew or pilot candidates, and does one of these: types them by physical indicators; models a physically driven outcome (G tolerance, injury, flight duty limitation); or builds a decision model for selection, assignment, training or fitness for duty.
- **4, directly useful.** Quantitative strength or fitness data on aircrew; machine-learning, typing, composite-scoring or decision-support methods in military or tactical populations, or built on strength indicators; authoritative standards for such models.
- **3, transferable.** Methods, measurements or frameworks from sport, clinical or general populations that adapt well. These are listed one line each below; the CSV has their full summaries.

## Limits

- **Chinese coverage.** The Chinese databases (CNKI, Wanfang) could not be reached from the search network, so Chinese coverage is limited to journals indexed in OpenAlex, Crossref and PubMed.
- **Grey literature.** NATO STO and DTIC full-text pages were blocked, so technical reports appear only where a database record exists.
- **Missing abstracts.** Fifteen listed papers have no abstract in any database. They are rated at most 3, summarised from the title, and marked as such.

## Leads that could not be confirmed (not in the list)

## Closest matches: physical indicators linked to pilot outcomes, types or assignment (15)

- **[Anatomical and functional asymmetry predicts G-force tolerance in high-Intensity physical performers](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-39250-8)**  
  Kyu-Lim Lee et al. (2026). *Scientific Reports*. [PDF](pdf/1_closest-matches_physical-indicators/5_Lee2026_Anatomical-and-functional-asymmetry-predicts-G-force-tolerance-in-high.pdf)  
  Thirty male air force cadets took a human-centrifuge G test and were assessed with the Functional Movement Screen, occlusal (bite) force distribution and skeletal muscle mass by bioelectrical impedance, analysed by pass/fail group comparison and multiple regression. The pass group had higher skeletal muscle mass and better FMS scores, the fail group showed greater left-right imbalances in FMS and occlusal force, and regression linked G-test outcome to occlusal force parameters.  
  *Use for your program:* Designing a G-tolerance suitability model: identifies candidate physical predictors (skeletal muscle mass, movement symmetry, occlusal force balance) in pilot candidates, though it uses no muscular-strength tests and rests on 30 cadets with no validation.

- **[Data analysis of civil aviation flight students' physical training based on ID3 decision tree and BP neural network](https://doi.org/10.1080/18824889.2026.2628263)**  
  Wei Song & Lina Wang (2026). *SICE Journal of Control, Measurement, and System Integration*. [Free full text](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/18824889.2026.2628263)  
  Applied an ID3 decision tree (information-gain feature selection and classification) followed by a back-propagation neural network to physical training data of civil aviation student pilots from the Physical Training Center of Civil Aviation University of China. The combined model reported 92% classification accuracy, 90% precision, 89% recall and 89.5% F1, beating the comparison algorithms, and identified muscle strength and endurance level as key features of fitness status; sample size is not stated.  
  *Use for your program:* Directly informs the pilot physical classification module: an ID3 decision-tree (information-gain feature selection) plus BP neural-network pipeline for flight students, which the authors present as combining interpretability with predictive power, and which identifies muscle strength and endurance as key classification features.  
  *Caution:* no held-out or external validation reported.

- **[Relationship between the G Tolerance, Physical Performance, and Cardiac Force Index in Male Aircrew: A Prospective Observational Study](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0000000000003763)**  
  Chung-Yu Lai et al. (2025). *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*. [PDF](pdf/1_closest-matches_physical-indicators/5_Lai2025_Relationship-between-the-G-Tolerance-Physical-Performance-and-Cardiac.pdf)  
  Male military flight cadets (53 low and 29 high G tolerance) had walking cardiac force index (WCFI) recorded with a wearable and a 100-m sprint timed ahead of high-G training, plus jump performance measured on the training day, and logistic regression related these to G tolerance. Higher WCFI (>0.15; aOR 3.72, 95% CI 1.10-12.60) went with better G tolerance and longer sprint time with poorer tolerance (aOR 0.38, 95% CI 0.15-0.95); the authors suggest sprint and jump tests plus cardiovascular monitoring to classify cadets into high or low G-tolerance groups for personalised training.  
  *Use for your program:* A statistical model in flight cadets that links pre-training field tests and a wearable cardiac index to G-tolerance group, with an explicit proposal to use such tests to sort cadets for personalised training. It is useful as typing and training-assignment logic, but the significant predictors were sprint time and WCFI, not muscular strength, and the abstract reports no jump-power result.

- **[Identifying contributory risk factors for neck pain in fast jet aircrew: a prospective cohort study](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00420-025-02162-7)**  
  James B. Wallace et al. (2025). *International Archives of Occupational and Environmental Health*. [PDF](pdf/1_closest-matches_physical-indicators/5_Wallace2025_Identifying-contributory-risk-factors-for-neck-pain-in-fast-jet.pdf)  
  Prospective cohort of 279 Royal Australian Air Force fast jet aircrew followed over four 5-month periods, using mixed-effects logistic regression of 44 baseline and 26 weekly measures on the weekly risk of neck-related musculoskeletal complaint episodes (320 new episodes). Previous neck pain, higher worry scores, larger acute flying workloads, more weekly flights and larger acute and chronic strength-and-conditioning workloads increased risk; absolute isometric neck-rotation and trunk-flexion strength, chronic flying workload and sleep quality had non-linear effects; and demographics, anthropometry, range of motion and S&C participation did not affect risk.  
  *Use for your program:* Injury-risk modelling and fitness-for-duty indicators: places isometric neck and trunk strength in a multivariable model of neck-complaint risk in aircrew.

- **[G Tolerance Prediction Model Using Mobile Device–Measured Cardiac Force Index for Military Aircrew: Observational Study](https://doi.org/10.2196/48812)**  
  Ming-Hao Kuo et al. (2023). *JMIR mHealth and uHealth*. [PDF](pdf/1_closest-matches_physical-indicators/5_Kuo2023_G-Tolerance-Prediction-Model-Using-Mobile-Device-Measured-Cardiac.pdf)  
  Recorded heart rate and activity with a wearable BioHarness in 213 military aircrew trainees undergoing high-G centrifuge training and derived a cardiac force index (weight x activity / heart rate). Multivariate regression gave formulas in which walking CFI, height and systolic blood pressure (plus age and heart rate for relaxed tolerance) predicted relaxed and straining G tolerance before training.  
  *Use for your program:* A worked example of a regression model predicting aircrew G tolerance from wearable and anthropometric measures, which informs prediction-model design; the model would still need validating.  
  *Caution:* no held-out or external validation reported.

- **[A Cardiac Force Index Applied to the G Tolerance Test and Surveillance among Male Military Aircrew](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph18168832)**  
  Kwo-Tsao Chiang et al. (2021). *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*. [PDF](pdf/1_closest-matches_physical-indicators/5_Chiang2021_A-Cardiac-Force-Index-Applied-to-the-G-Tolerance-Test-and-Surveillance.pdf)  
  In 92 male military aircrew, a wearable (BioHarness 3.0) recorded heart rate and activity at rest and while walking to compute a cardiac force index (CFI = weight x activity/HR), which was related to relaxed and straining G tolerance measured in a centrifuge. Each 100-unit rise in walking CFI raised relaxed G tolerance by 0.14 G and straining G tolerance by 0.17 G, and higher walking CFI increased the chance of exceeding 5 G relaxed and 8 G straining tolerance.  
  *Use for your program:* A worked example of a simple wearable-derived composite index used to predict centrifuge G tolerance in aircrew, informing composite scoring and automated G-tolerance screening.

- **[Roles of Physiological Responses and Anthropometric Factors on the Gravitational Force Tolerance for Occupational Hypergravity Exposure](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17218061)**  
  Min-Yu Tu et al. (2020). *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health*. [PDF](pdf/1_closest-matches_physical-indicators/5_Tu2020_Roles-of-Physiological-Responses-and-Anthropometric-Factors-on-the.pdf)  
  Retrospectively reviewed high-G training records of 873 student pilots, 44 (5.04%) of whom could not sustain 7.5 G for 15 s, to identify physiological and anthropometric correlates of G tolerance. A heart-rate increase under 10% in the first 1-5 s was linked to nearly ten-fold higher odds of failure (adjusted OR 9.91, 95% CI 4.11-23.88), and BMI, relaxed and straining G tolerance and AGSM were reported as negatively correlated with the outcome.  
  *Use for your program:* Models G-tolerance failure risk in student pilots from physiological and anthropometric factors, directly informing the G-tolerance risk-prediction part of the program.

- **[Fighter pilots’ physical performance and spinal-injury induced flight duty limitations](http://urn.fi/URN:ISBN:978-951-39-7723-8)**  
  Tuomas Honkanen (2019). *Jyväskylä University Digital Archive (University of Jyväskylä)*. [PDF](pdf/1_closest-matches_physical-indicators/5_Honkanen2019_Fighter-pilots-physical-performance-and-spinal-injury-induced-flight.pdf)  
  Doctoral thesis combining four studies of fighter pilots on fitness tests, lumbar muscle cross-sectional area, Gz exposure and neck/shoulder EMG in relation to low back pain and spinal-disorder-induced flight duty limitation (FDL). Selection-phase pull-up and back-extension results were associated with later FDL and isometric back endurance with activity-related low back pain, whereas aerobic fitness and early-career cumulative Gz exposure were not associated with FDL and muscle CSA was not associated with low back pain.  
  *Use for your program:* Supports using selection-phase axial strength and endurance tests as predictive indicators of spinal-injury flight duty limitation in the program's selection and fitness-for-duty modules; its sub-studies overlap the author's journal articles on functional tests and LBP and on selection-phase muscular fitness and FDL, which may be preferable to cite.  
  *Caution:* thesis.

- **[Constitutional Traits of the Statokinetic Stability of Flying Personnel under the Conditions of Long Navy Trip](https://doi.org/10.1134/s0362119719070053)**  
  L. A. Glaznikov et al. (2019). *Human Physiology*.  
  Developed two discriminant functions from anthropometric indices in 220 subjects to identify the digestive and muscular somatotypes (accuracy 96% and 87%), then grouped 42 aircraft-carrier pilots by dominant somatotype and measured statokinetic stability (tolerance of cumulative Coriolis acceleration) during a long navy deployment. Pilots of the muscular somatotype had the highest statokinetic stability and those of the digestive somatotype the lowest.  
  *Use for your program:* The closest published example of typing pilots by physique with a discriminant classifier and linking the types to an aviation-relevant tolerance outcome, a template for strength-based typing.

- **[Assessment of Muscular Fitness as a Predictor of Flight Duty Limitation](https://doi.org/10.1093/milmed/usy089)**  
  Tuomas Honkanen et al. (2018). *Military Medicine*.  
  Retrospectively compared Finnish Air Force pilot-selection-phase anthropometry, aerobic ergometer results and a five-test muscular fitness battery between 23 pilots flying with Gz limitation due to spinal disorders and 50 experienced symptom-free controls. Non-limited pilots had better selection-phase pull-up (14.4 vs 11.5) and back-extension (71.1 vs 60.0) results, a higher total muscular fitness score (13.7 vs 12.4) and more competitive-sport participation (54% vs 22%), while aerobic fitness and anthropometry did not differ.  
  *Use for your program:* Supports choosing selection-phase axial-strength tests (pull-ups, back extensions) and a composite muscular fitness score as candidate indicators of later spinal-disorder flight duty limitation for the selection and fitness-for-duty modules; the evidence is a retrospective group comparison (23 limited vs 50 controls), not a validated screening rule.

- **[Functional test measures as risk indicators for low back pain among fixed-wing military pilots](https://doi.org/10.1136/jramc-2015-000510)**  
  Tuomas Honkanen et al. (2016). *Journal of the Royal Army Medical Corps*.  
  Prospective study of 104 male fixed-wing military pilots who completed a functional fitness test battery and a questionnaire at baseline, with LBP reassessed by questionnaire 5 years later. Isometric low back endurance was associated with physical activity-related LBP at follow-up, but the functional test results were not associated with flight-related LBP (baseline prevalence 71% overall, 31% flight-related).  
  *Use for your program:* Informs the injury-risk and fitness-for-duty parts of the program: among the pilot functional fitness tests only isometric back endurance was associated with later LBP (activity-related, not flight-related), which cautions against relying on this battery to screen for flight-related LBP risk.

- **[Unpredictability of Fighter Pilots’ G Duration Tolerance by Anthropometric and Physiological Characteristics](https://doi.org/10.3357/amhp.4032.2015)**  
  Myunghwan Park et al. (2015). *Aerospace Medicine and Human Performance*.  
  Thirty-eight subjects (fighter pilots, per the title) wearing anti-G suits and performing AGSM were held at a +7.3 G centrifuge plateau until loss of peripheral vision (mean tolerance time 31.6 s, range 20-58 s), and their tolerance time was tested against anthropometric and physiological factors using correlation, stepwise multiple regression, t-tests on dichotomised factors and logistic regression. No factor correlated significantly and the regression showed no dependence on any personal factor; height was inversely associated in the dichotomised t-test, but logistic regression found the effect too weak to predict tolerance, so G-duration tolerance could not be predicted from these characteristics.  
  *Use for your program:* Informs the aptitude-classification and G-tolerance parts of the program as a cautionary null result: physical typing by anthropometric or physiological traits should not be assumed to predict sustained-G duration tolerance without validation. The abstract does not list the factors tested, so check the full text for whether any strength measures were included.

- **[Acceleration risk in student fighter pilots: preliminary analysis of a management program.](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/15619864)**  
  Samuel M. Galvagno et al. (2004). *Aviation, space, and environmental medicine*.  
  Describes the G-Risk Indicator Management (GRIM) Program at Luke AFB, in which anthropometric data, previous G-performance, anaerobic fitness and centrifuge qualification scores are used to qualitatively determine an F-16 student pilot's G-risk level and to aid tailored ground training. Significant differences between groups (not defined in the abstract) were found for anaerobic test scores, centrifuge scores and gradebook comments, but without historical controls no conclusion on the program's efficiency could be drawn.  
  *Use for your program:* An operational example of combining anthropometric, fitness and centrifuge indicators into a pilot G-risk level that guides tailored ground training, the pattern of the decision-support module; because the risk level is assigned qualitatively and was not validated, it also shows the validation gap to close.  
  *Caution:* published before 2005.

- **[Prediction of Anthropometric Accommodation in Aircraft Cockpits](https://doi.org/10.21236/ada399750)**  
  Gregory F. Zehner (2001). [PDF](pdf/1_closest-matches_physical-indicators/5_Zehner2001_Prediction-of-Anthropometric-Accommodation-in-Aircraft-Cockpits.pdf)  
  A dissertation, issued as a report, that sets out a systematic method for predicting pilots' cockpit accommodation from anthropometric data: define the user population, set functional requirements (see, reach and actuate controls; external vision; safe escape), test the population's ability to meet them, and derive predictive equations for selecting future users. It demonstrates the method on the USAF T-38A, prompted by the USAF considering relaxing body-size entry requirements for flight training; the abstract gives no sample size or accuracy figures.  
  *Use for your program:* It is a worked template for the aircraft-type suitability and selection decision-support part of the program, showing how body measurements can be linked to aircraft-specific functional requirements and turned into predictive selection equations, which could be extended with strength indicators.  
  *Caution:* technical or government report; thesis; published before 2005.

- **[USN/USMC Ejection Seat Equipped Aircraft Anthropometric Accommodation](https://doi.org/10.21236/ada377912)**  
  Greg Kennedy (1999). *Naval Air Systems Command technical report (DTIC ADA377912)*. [PDF](pdf/1_closest-matches_physical-indicators/5_Kennedy1999_USN-USMC-Ejection-Seat-Equipped-Aircraft-Anthropometric-Accommodation.pdf)  
  Naval Air Systems Command report that replaced outdated anthropometric accommodation guidance for US Navy and Marine Corps ejection-seat aircraft with a multivariate statistical accommodation envelope from a baseline assessment of in-service aircraft. The revised guidance adds pipeline relational charting so candidate aviators can be assigned to a correct and safe training pipeline, avoiding the cost of reassignment.  
  *Use for your program:* An operational precedent for classifying aviator candidates by physical measures and using the result to assign them to aircraft pipelines, the same logic a strength-based type classification would follow.  
  *Caution:* technical or government report; published before 2005.

## Closest matches: decision models for pilot selection and assignment (5)

- **[Responsible machine learning for United States Air Force pilot candidate selection](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dss.2024.114198)**  
  Devin Wasilefsky et al. (2024). *Decision Support Systems*. [Free full text](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S0167923624000319) (author's accepted manuscript)  
  Proposes a decision-support system for US Air Force pilot candidate selection built on interpretable and explainable machine learning, in line with the DoD Responsible AI Strategy: Gaussian Bayesian networks and black-box models explained with SHAP values and conformal prediction, trained on 2010-2018 USAF data to predict candidates' merit assignment selection system scores from information available before training. A preferred pair of models was embedded in the Air Force Pilot Applicant Selection System.  
  *Use for your program:* The most direct precedent for the decision-support layer: an explainable, uncertainty-aware machine-learning selection tool for pilot candidates, embedded in an operational selection system.

- **[Attrition Risk and Aircraft Suitability Prediction in U.S. Navy Pilot Training Using Machine Learning](https://doi.org/10.3390/aerospace10040379)**  
  Jubilee Prasad-Rao et al. (2023). *Aerospace*. [PDF](pdf/2_closest-matches_decision-models/5_Prasad-Rao2023_Attrition-Risk-and-Aircraft-Suitability-Prediction-in-U-S-Navy-Pilot_author-manuscript.pdf) (author's accepted manuscript)  
  Applied feature extraction and machine learning to sparse flight-test grades from about 10 years of U.S. Navy pilot training records to flag student aviators at high risk of late-stage attrition, catching 50% of attrition with a 4% false-positive rate. Separate models were trained to recommend a suitable training aircraft type for each student aviator.  
  *Use for your program:* A direct template for the decision-support layer: it turns trainee performance records into attrition-risk flags and aircraft-type assignment recommendations (the predictors are training grades, not physical measures).  
  *Caution:* no held-out or external validation reported.  
  *Note:* The published version is open access on the publisher's site (title link); the saved PDF is the authors' manuscript.

- **[Predicting success in United States Air Force pilot training using machine learning techniques](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.seps.2021.101121)**  
  Phillip R. Jenkins et al. (2022). *Socio-Economic Planning Sciences*. [Free full text](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/am/pii/S0038012121001130) (author's accepted manuscript)  
  Tested several machine-learning techniques and hyperparameter settings on historical records of US Air Force specialized undergraduate pilot training candidates to predict training success. Extremely randomized trees reached nearly 94% accuracy, and degree type and commissioning source were the most important features.  
  *Use for your program:* A USAF precedent for machine-learning prediction of pilot-training success to inform selection criteria, the predictive core of a selection decision-support tool.

- **[Personality Traits in Selection of Military, Civil and Sports' Pilots: Hybridized-IT2FS-MCDM Approach](https://doi.org/10.7708/ijtte2022.12(1).01)**  
  Ivan Petrović & Jelena Petrović (2021). *INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL FOR TRAFFIC AND TRANSPORT ENGINEERING*. [PDF](pdf/2_closest-matches_decision-models/5_Petrovic2021_Personality-Traits-in-Selection-of-Military-Civil-and-Sports-Pilots.pdf)  
  Builds a pilot-selection model that uses interval type-2 fuzzy DEMATEL and AHP to screen and weight personality traits and fuzzy TOPSIS to rank candidates, based on an online survey in which 72 pilots participated. The most important traits were responsible and cooperative for commercial pilots, determined and responsible for military pilots, and sociable and determined for sports pilots.  
  *Use for your program:* A worked fuzzy MCDM template (criteria screening, weighting, candidate ranking, role-specific weights) for the selection decision-support layer, transferable to physical-fitness criteria.

- **[Study on the analytic hierarchy process of the medical selection for high performance fighter pilot](https://doi.org/10.3760/cma.j.issn.1007-6239.2018.03-04.002)** [ZH]  
  Cong Wang et al. (2018). *Chinese Journal of Aerospace Medicine*.  
  Built an analytic hierarchy process (AHP) model for medical selection of high-performance fighter pilots from aeromedical expert judgement, with 5 first-level indexes (basic physiological function 0.1728, flight physiological endurance 0.2241, psychological basis 0.1785, mental ability 0.2891, physical fitness and ergonomics 0.1355) and 23 second-level indexes. Applied to 16 candidates, it produced composite scores ranging from 7.79 down to 5.20.  
  *Use for your program:* Informs composite scoring and selection decision-support design: an expert-weighted AHP hierarchy that places physical fitness and ergonomics alongside physiological, psychological and mental-ability indexes to rank fighter pilot candidates.  
  *Caution:* fewer than 30 participants.

## Pilot and aircrew strength, fitness and flight demands (117)

### Directly useful

- **[Structural and functional asymmetry of the lower limb related to gravitational acceleration tolerance](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2025.114506)**  
  Jun-Young Sung et al. (2026). *iScience*. [PDF](pdf/3_pilot-strength-and-fitness/4_Sung2026_Structural-and-functional-asymmetry-of-the-lower-limb-related-to.pdf)  
  Thirty male air force cadets, grouped as pass or fail on a G (acceleration tolerance) test, were compared on femoral cartilage thickness by ultrasound, isokinetic knee flexor/extensor strength and the Functional Movement Screen (FMS). The fail group had thinner right medial-condyle cartilage and more knee-flexion asymmetry at 180°/s, while the pass group had better FMS scores, greater muscular strength and less limb asymmetry.  
  *Use for your program:* Indicator selection: supports isokinetic knee strength and left-right limb asymmetry (with FMS) as candidate indicators associated with G-test pass/fail in air force cadets. The evidence is a cross-sectional comparison of 30 cadets, not a predictive or typing model.

- **[Does body composition influence air force pilots’ resistance to high-G acceleration? A systematic review meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2026.115710)**  
  Kyu-Lim Lee et al. (2026). *iScience*. [PDF](pdf/3_pilot-strength-and-fitness/4_Lee2026_Does-body-composition-influence-air-force-pilots-resistance-to-high-G.pdf)  
  Systematic review and meta-analysis of ten studies (published since 2014) on whether body composition is associated with G-test pass/fail outcomes in pilots and cadets. Pooled effect sizes were moderate for BMI (0.47) and small for body weight (0.26) and muscle mass (0.31), with low-to-moderate heterogeneity, while physical fitness variables did not differ between pass and fail groups.  
  *Use for your program:* Choosing and weighting indicators for G-tolerance suitability: pooled evidence that BMI, body weight and muscle mass are modestly associated with G-test pass/fail, while the fitness variables studied did not differ. This is a caution against typing built only on fitness test results.

- **[Science behind policy: implementing a modern circumference-based body fat equation with a physical fitness threshold is associated with lower musculoskeletal injury risk](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41366-024-01701-5)**
